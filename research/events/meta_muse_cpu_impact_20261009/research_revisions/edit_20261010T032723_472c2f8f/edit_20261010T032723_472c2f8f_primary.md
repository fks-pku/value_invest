L23: By going into detail about how Muse works under the hood, we hope to give you a sense of how — and how much — you can trust it in practice. The following describes the system at launch. We will, of course, continue to pay attention to the changing threat landscape and make changes as required.
L24: ## The Lay of the Land
L25: 
L26: ### An agent in your own cloud computer
L27: 
L28: You and your Muse share your own dedicated computer in the cloud. This is where your Muse lives and where all data and credentials for any service you connect are securely stored. Each Virtual Machine(VM) is an isolated linux box with a browser and enough storage, CPU, and memory to do real work — like compiling code the agent writes, developing custom skills and handling concurrent sub-agents and crons.
L29: ### Your computer, your data
L30: 
L31: Your dedicated VM is the system of record for everything you put in Muse. Muse sends limited data out of the VM when necessary for inference and telemetry as described below.
L32: 
L33: ### Clients
L34: 
L35: Muse clients (the iOS and Android apps, the web UI) connect directly to your VM via a secure transport layer.
L36: ### Connectors
L37: Muse is most useful when you connect it to your email and other systems like your car or your home. We’ve built an initial set of connectors to third-party systems and to other Meta apps like Instagram and Facebook. For each connector, we worked closely with the service provider to integrate their API, and we’ve written and iterated on SKILLs — detailed instructions to Muse — on how to get the most out of each connector.
L38: Muse can also write its own custom connectors for other services you care about if they have their own APIs or CLIs.
L39: ## Muse Secure VM
L40: 
L41: The VM is structured carefully to separate what you and your Muse do from everything we’ve built in to keep you safe using Linux isolation primitives. The right mental model is two isolated security domains on one box, not an LLM powered agent with root.
L42: The Hatch^{cite3†1 Hatch is our internal name for Muse in the codebase.} daemon (the core agentic harness) as well as the filesystem containing your workspace and files, and all the binaries and tools Muse executes on your behalf operate in a `systemd-nspawn` runtime container. Root inside the runtime cell is mapped to an unprivileged host user so runtime cell root is not host root.
L43: The cell gets its own root filesystem (including a full debian image) separate from the host filesystem where your other more sensitive data lives. It also gets a virtual network interface, filtered system calls (for example no `io_uring`), and limited kernel capabilities (for example no `CAP_SYS_PTRACE` and no `CAP_NET_ADMIN`).

L249: ## What Is Compute Power?
L250: 
L251: Simply put, compute power is the measure of how much work a computer chip can do and how fast it can do it — like horsepower in a car engine. Compute power is measured in FLOPS: floating-point operations per second, or the number of calculations that a chip can perform in one second. FLOPS measure the speed of compute and gigawatts measure the scale of it, or how many chips you can keep running at once.
L252: When you ask Meta AI to find a vegan restaurant, it runs billions of calculations in just a few seconds. Your voice is captured, converted from sound waves into text, and routed to computers or servers inside a cite86†data center . From there, a large language model (LLM), and the result is delivered right to your ear.
L253: Even simple actions, searching for a local barbershop on Instagram, require layers of computation: understanding language, processing your query, scanning an index, generating results, and delivering it back to you, all before your thumb leaves the screen. All of this processing power is made possible by processing chips inside the servers inside our data centers.

L15: ## How We Trained Muse Glimmer
L16: 
L17: An agent that manages your schedule, drafts your messages, organizes your files, and learns how you work needs deep access to personal context. It also needs several capabilities working in concert: long-horizon execution, precise tool calling, multimodal understanding, long-context memory, and instruction following.
L18: We designed Muse Glimmer to balance capability against the memory and compute constraints of local hardware. This required a compact architecture, a novel distillation recipe that transfers agentic reasoning from a much larger teacher model, and inference optimizations — including quantization — to meet latency expectations. We achieved this in the following phases:
L19:   * Pre-Training. We trained Muse Glimmer on Muse Spark's outputs using logit distillation, leveraging a similar data mix as the teacher.
L20:   * Mid-Training. We trained the model on longer-context, more agent-heavy data with richer reasoning traces, alongside organic data.
L21:   * Post-Training. We combined supervised fine-tuning with a mix of on-policy distillation and reinforcement learning across general, reasoning, coding, and agentic domains.
L22: Muse Glimmer was evaluated under the standards set out in cite5†Meta's Advanced AI Scaling Framework†ai.meta.com and assessed for open-weight release across all relevant categories.

L44: ## Optimized for Local Deployments
L45: 
L46: A local agent is truly useful if it's fast enough to feel responsive. An agent that takes minutes to reply or plan its next step breaks the flow of real work. We applied two optimizations to make Muse Glimmer run at practical speeds on consumer hardware without sacrificing quality.
L47: ### Fitting the Model on Your Device.
L48: At full precision, a 30-billion parameter model would require over 55 GB of memory — far more than any consumer GPU offers. We use quantization techniques to compress the model's weights to approximately 4-bit precision, shrinking the language model to under 20 GB. This leaves enough headroom for the model's working memory (its "KV cache"), the perception encoder for image understanding, and the speculative decoding drafter to run simultaneously within a 24 GB or 32 GB envelope.
L49: We validated that this compression introduces minimal to no degradation on agentic tasks.
L50: ### Faster Generation Through Speculative Decoding.
L51: Language models normally generate text one token at a time, which can feel slow during long reasoning chains or multi-step tool calls. Muse Glimmer ships with a lightweight "drafter" model based on cite7†DFlash†arxiv.org — a small companion network that proposes entire blocks of tokens at once. The main model then verifies these proposals in parallel, accepting correct tokens and correcting wrong ones.
L52: This technique lets Muse Glimmer generate text significantly faster than standard token-by-token generation while producing identical output quality. We provide quantized drafter versions to incur a smaller memory overhead in the release.
