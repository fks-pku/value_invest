Overview
The Web3 ecosystem in 2025 entered a period of renewed activity, driven by a combination of favorable 
macroeconomic conditions, improving market sentiment, and a markedly more crypto-friendly political 
climate in the United States. The new U.S. Administration signaled early that digital assets would be 
treated as a strategic innovation sector rather than a regulatory outlier, restoring confidence among 
builders and investors. Additionally, decentralized applications broadened their reach into payments, 
gaming, tokenized assets, and identity, demonstrating crypto’s utility in everyday activities. This 
resurgence in growth, however, was matched by an equally active threat landscape as adversaries 
refined both technical and social engineering tactics. 
Year-over-year comparisons between 2025 and 2024 illustrate the shifting nature of risk. Total losses 
in 2025 amounted to $3,352,850,816, versus $2,446,285,251  in 2024, representing an approximate 
37.0 6% increase. However, when isolating the impact of the Bybit incident, which accounted for a 
disproportionately large share of annual losses at $1,447 ,063,421, the industry would have actually 
recorded a net decrease in funds stolen compared to 2025. This contrast highlights a key trend: 
while the frequency of smaller attacks remains persistent, adversaries are increasingly concentrating 
resources into fewer, but significantly larger, high-impact operations. The Bybit exploit signals that well-
capitalized, well-coordinated threat actors are becoming more active across the ecosystem. 
Additionally, when isolating the Bybit impact (which we include in the Supply Chain category of attack 
vectors), phishing becomes the top attack vector with $722,885,398 stolen across 248 incidents. 
Code vulnerabilities closely follows, with $554,646,929 stolen in 240 incidents. In the case of code 
vulnerabilities, however, more than 47% of funds was either frozen or returned, such as in the Cetus 
incident, which we detail later in this report.

Artificial intelligence became one of the defining forces in Web3 security this year, which is used by 
both attackers and their defenders. On the defensive side, developers increasingly used AI-assisted 
tooling to generate tests, identify contract inefficiencies, and improve audit workflows. On the offensive 
side, however, attackers also adopted AI at scale. Notable trends included the following: 
 ↗ AI-generated phishing websites and wallet pop-ups that were nearly indistinguishable from 
authentic interfaces. 
 ↗ Automated multi-lingual phishing campaigns, which we discuss in detail in a later section. 
 ↗ AI-powered reconnaissance, where threat actors scrape on-chain activity and chatroom 
conversations to identify high-value targets. 
 ↗ More realistic impersonation attacks, such as fake founder accounts or deepfakes. 
 ↗ Rapid exploit replication, with attackers using AI tools to produce copycat attacks within short time 
periods. 
In 2025, regulatory clarity also continued to improve worldwide. In the U.S., the GENIUS Act set early 
frameworks for digital asset transparency and stablecoin oversight, while signaling a more cooperative 
stance toward innovation. This shift, combined with clearer tax guidance and custody rules, provided 
developers and institutions with long-awaited predictability. 
These developments, among others, moved the Web3 ecosystem toward more structured and 
harmonized governance, which in turn influenced how projects approached design, compliance, and 
operational security. 
As 2026 approaches, several trends appear likely to define the next phase of Web3 security. Attackers 
are expected to deepen their use of AI-driven impersonation and large-scale phishing operations. 
Supply chain attacks may increase in sophistication as adversaries target infrastructure providers and 
CI/CD pipelines. At the same time, maturing regulatory frameworks, enhanced real-time monitoring, and 
the broader adoption of AI-assisted defense tools may mitigate some categories of preventable risk. 
The landscape is evolving quickly, and the projects that succeed will be those that integrate security 
into every operational decision, from architecture to user experience.

Phishing Was 2025’s Most
Common Attack Vector
Although phishing was not the highest-value attack vector of the year, it accounted for the largest 
number of recorded incidents at 248, surpassing both supply chain breaches and code vulnerabilities. 
In total, $722,885,398 was stolen from phishing. This reinforces a persistent reality across Web3 
security: attackers continue to favor low-cost, high-volume techniques that exploit human trust rather 
than protocol logic or technical vulnerabilities. 
It is important to note that our phishing figures are almost certainly conservative. Many phishing-related 
incidents go unreported, especially when losses are small, spread across individual users, or tied to 
scams that fall outside traditional “hack” classifications. Our dataset in this report does not include pig 
butchering schemes, wrench attacks, high-pressure investment scams, or social engineering fraud 
conducted entirely off-chain, all of which remain widespread across the broader crypto landscape. As 
reporting standards improve and more ecosystems integrate formal incident disclosures, we expect 
future editions of Hack3D to capture a more comprehensive view of phishing-related losses. 
In contrast to supply chain compromises, which remain resource-intensive and rely on breaching 
infrastructure, phishing requires minimal investment. The barrier to entry is extremely low. Attackers 
can reproduce proven playbooks, iterate quickly, and reach large audiences in minutes.

1. Treat Every Wallet Prompt as High-Risk:  
Always verify the domain, contract, and action being requested. Phishing attacks succeed through 
signature prompts disguised as routine approvals. 
2. Use Multi-Factor Signing Policies:  
Deploy hardware wallets, multi-signature requirements, or transaction simulation tools to introduce 
friction before funds can move. 
3. Never Rely on Private Messages for Support:  
No legitimate project provides support via unsolicited DMs. Users should assume all inbound 
messages are malicious until proven otherwise. 
4. Confirm Announcements Across Official Channels:  
Cross-verify updates in Discord, Telegram, X, GitHub, and official websites before interacting with 
new contracts or “urgent” upgrades. 
5. Monitor Allowances and Revoke Regularly:  
Continuous allowance monitoring reduces the blast radius of a compromised signature. 
6. Educate Teams on Social Engineering Tactics:  
For projects, team-level training and standardized communication policies significantly reduce 
internal phishing risks, especially during major upgrades or emergencies. 
7. Use Endpoint Security and Anti-Phishing Protection:  
Phishing often begins outside of Web3 itself, through compromised email, browsers, or infected 
extensions. Traditional cybersecurity hygiene remains essential.

This strategy involves enticing users to divert from the official websites of influential platforms to

tokens and NFTs worth millions of dollars.

Bybit Incident Analysis: 
$1.4 Billion Lost
On February 21, 2025, Bybit Exchange suffered the largest crypto theft to date, resulting in the loss of 
approximately $1.4 billion. 
The attack was carried out by the Lazarus Group, a North Korean state-sponsored hacking group. 
Instead of attacking Bybit directly, the hackers targeted Safe{Wallet}, a third-party multi-signature 
wallet provider used by Bybit to secure critical transactions. They infiltrated the system through 
a Safe{Wallet} developer's compromised machine and injected malicious JavaScript code into the 
wallet management interface. This code covertly altered transactions approved by Bybit employees, 
redirecting funds to the hackers despite the multi-signature process that requires multiple human 
approvals.
Once the funds were withdrawn, the attackers quickly dispersed the stolen assets across various 
wallets, blockchains, and decentralized exchanges to obscure the trail. Bybit's internal security systems 
were not breached; the attack exploited the user interface of the Safe{Wallet} system, leading signers 
to unknowingly approve malicious transactions. This sophisticated manipulation allowed the Lazarus 
Group to bypass multi-signature wallet protections. Bybit has since been working with industry experts 
to trace the stolen assets and has launched a recovery bounty program.
For a more detailed analysis of the Bybit incident, read our blog, Bybit Incident Technical Analysis .

According to researcher ZachXBT, a BTC whale was the target of a social engineering attack in April 
2025, resulting in the loss of approximately $330 million. The attacker laundered funds via six instant

pool mechanisms. 
By deploying spoof tokens and manipulating price curves, the hacker was able to extract substantial 
funds from the platform. Fortunately, Sui validators managed to freeze and return $162 million  of the 
stolen assets, following a governance proposal for user repayment.

Balancer: $113 Million Lost
17

MEASURING RISK AMID RISING TVL
On November 3, 2025, Balancer protocol and its forks, Beets and Bex, were hit by an exploit that caused 
an initial total loss of around $130 million. The attack was caused by a precision flaw in Balancer’s batch 
swap logic. The attacker was able to leverage this vulnerability to manipulate the liquidity pool pricing 
model, artificially increasing the internal token balance held by their contract. After inflating these 
balances through repeated swaps, they withdrew the funds using the manageUserBalance() function.
In total, Balancer suffered about $113 million in losses, while Beets and Bex were affected by roughly 
$3.8 million and $12.4 million, respectively. Although the initial losses were close to $130 million, this 
amount has so far been reduced to around $96.4 million, thanks to asset freezes and returns by white 
hat actors.
For a more detailed analysis of the Balancer incident, read our blog, Balancer Incident Analysis .

Individual User Risks and
Mitigation
Apart from the major incidents mentioned above, in 2025, attackers increasingly shifted their focus 
toward individual users, where defenses are often weaker and losses remain underreported. Many 
types of scams have little to no reporting around them, such as pig butchering and investment scams. 
With the rise of AI, phishing techniques have become more advanced, leveraging deepfakes and voice 
spoofing. Wrench attacks targeting crypto holders also surged in 2025, fueled by millions of holder 
identities leaked from exchange databases and combined with location information.
The best protection starts with education: understanding the main types of attacks and staying up-
to-date through reliable resources. From there, holders should segment their assets across multiple 
wallets with different levels of exposure so that the compromise of a single key or account never puts all 
their funds at risk. This approach must be complemented by strong access controls (unique and strong 
passwords, password manager, 2FA). Finally, the user must limit what they expose publicly, and verify 
every URL, address, and permission before signing.
